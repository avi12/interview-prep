## The question you always get

"Tell me about yourself."

It is not a request for your life story. The interviewer wants a **map**: who you are, what you can do, why you are here.

- Aim for **90 seconds**. Guides say 90 to 120
- It is an elevator pitch: "short" and "direct"
- You only need the summary. Their questions fill in the rest

> **Not this:** your life in order, from school to today. The Tech Interview Handbook says it plainly: "avoid a history lesson".

```narrate
Tell me about yourself is the first question in almost every interview. It is not a request for your life story. The interviewer wants a map: who you are, what you can do, and why you are here. Aim for ninety seconds. Give the summary, and let their questions fill in the rest.
```

```narrate
You have ninety seconds. [[punch]] Start with the punchline: one sentence that says who you are now, like a lab technician turned backend developer. That takes about ten seconds. [[present]] Then the present: what you build or study today, and one result with a number. About thirty seconds. [[past]] Then the past: your old job, told as proof of skills this job needs. [[future]] End with the future: why this role at this company.
```

> **Why:** the **punchline first** gives the interviewer something to follow up on. The Tech Interview Handbook: "tell them the executive summary" and let their interest drive the questions.

## Your old job is proof

Do not apologize for the change. Say what it gives you.

- Weak opening: "I am trying to break into tech"
- Strong opening: "I have spent years working in a field where I understand the problems deeply" (Mentor Cruise)
- Hiring managers for juniors look for **curiosity** and clear **communication** (LeadDev)

| Your old work | Say it proves | Ads that ask for it |
|---|---|---|
| Customer service, retail | You explain things to non-technical people | Analyst: stakeholders, 84% of 99 ads |
| Lab, finance, research | You are exact, and know a domain | Analyst: attention to detail, 48% |
| Any team job | Communication, teamwork | Backend: 41 of 45 ads |
| Studying alone to switch | You learn fast | Backend: learning mindset, 31 of 45 |

```narrate
Do not apologize for your career change. Say what it gives you. A weak opening is, I am trying to break into tech. A stronger one starts from experience: I spent years in a field where I understand the problems deeply. Hiring managers for juniors look for curiosity and clear communication. And the job ads ask for exactly the skills your old work already proves.
```

## Fill in the template

```text
Punchline  I am a ___ (old job) turned ___ (new role) who ___ (what you build).
Present    Right now I ___ (project or course). Last month I ___ (one result, with a number).
Past       Before that, ___ years as ___. There I learned ___, which matters here because ___.
Future     I want this role at ___ because ___ (their product, data or stack).
```

- One sentence per line. Short sentences are easier to say when you are nervous
- Write bullets, not a script. Practice until it sounds natural, not memorized
- Keep a number in **Present**

```narrate
Here is the template. Four lines: punchline, present, past, future. Fill each blank with your own words. Use one short sentence per line, because short sentences are easier to say when you are nervous. Keep one number in the present part. Write bullets, not a script, and practice until it sounds natural instead of memorized.
```

## A weak answer and a strong one

**Weak.** "So, um, I studied biology, then I worked in a lab, and then I wasn't happy, so I started learning to code online. I'm trying to break into tech. I'm looking for any opportunity."

**Strong.** "I am a lab technician turned backend developer who builds data tools. Right now I run a Python and Postgres project that loads and checks 40,000 rows of lab data. Before that I spent six years in a lab, where I learned to be exact and to explain results to people who are not scientists. I want this role because your team works with data, and I already know that side."

> **Trap:** the weak answer starts with history, apologizes, has no proof, and never mentions the company.

```quiz
q: Which one is a punchline?
options:
  - "So, I studied biology and then I worked in a lab..."
  - "I am a lab technician turned backend developer who builds data tools."
  - "I am trying to break into tech."
answer: 1
why: A punchline names who you are now and what you do. The first starts a history, the third apologizes.
```

```narrate
Compare these two. The weak one starts with history, says um, apologizes, and ends with any opportunity. It has no proof and never mentions the company. The strong one opens with who you are, gives one project with a number, turns the old job into skills, and ends on why this team. Same person, same story. Only the order and the proof changed.
```

## Hebrew and English

Interviews in Israel are usually in Hebrew, with English tech terms (lesson 01). Some firms switch to English.

Prepare **both**. Same four parts. Tech words stay English in both.

| English | עברית |
|---|---|
| I am a lab technician turned backend developer. | אני טכנאי מעבדה שהפך למפתח בק-אנד. |
| Right now I build a Python and Postgres project. | כרגע אני בונה פרויקט בפייתון ו-Postgres. |
| I want this role because your team works with data. | אני רוצה את התפקיד כי הצוות שלכם עובד עם נתונים. |

> **Why:** you will not know the language until the call starts. If both versions are ready, you do not freeze.

```narrate
Interviews in Israel are usually in Hebrew, with English technical words. Some firms switch to English. So prepare both versions, with the same four parts. Write the Hebrew one first if that is easier, then say the English one out loud, in short sentences. Then you are ready for whichever language comes.
```

## Say it out loud

1. Fill the template with your real story.
2. Set a timer for 90 seconds. Record yourself on your phone.
3. Listen once. Check three things:
   - Is the punchline in the first sentence?
   - Is there one number?
   - Does it end on **this** company?
4. Record again. Save the best take.

Do it in Hebrew, then in English.
